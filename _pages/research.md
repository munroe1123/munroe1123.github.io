## Publication

**[Complex Institutions, Social Identities, and Belief Formation](https://www.emerald.com/jpipe/article/7/1/73/1364114?casa_token=cJ_AMS4KUQcAAAAA:0gwnH5VLtwQomcUtowKGloEYm3ty518CHKc3qKLptN9s1nUPcq2Em8R0RxOgeaPXwSIeGfQnTVZVdz3z)**  
Carlo M. Horz and Munroe Kim  
*Journal of Political Institutions and Political Economy*, 7(1): 73–94, 2026.  
[Published Version](https://www.emerald.com/jpipe/article/7/1/73/1364114?casa_token=cJ_AMS4KUQcAAAAA:0gwnH5VLtwQomcUtowKGloEYm3ty518CHKc3qKLptN9s1nUPcq2Em8R0RxOgeaPXwSIeGfQnTVZVdz3z)

<details>
  <summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>

  <p>
  Do social identities impede citizens’ ability to form accurate beliefs about politics when policy outcomes are generated by complex institutions? We study this question theoretically and experimentally. In our formal model, citizens do not know the competence of the officeholder and they do not know whether or not the opposition enjoys veto powers, but they do observe policy outcomes. After characterizing Bayesian beliefs, we hypothesize that identity concerns cause upward (downward) biased beliefs among citizens who are aligned with the office-holder (opposition) for the competence of the officeholder. In our experiment, we find that identities do not affect beliefs about competence. However, identities affect beliefs about opposition veto power: for subjects aligned with the incumbent, identities have a negative effect when there is good news about policy; for subjects aligned with the opposition, identity concerns have a negative effect when there is bad news about policy.
  </p>
</details>

**Media Bias from Social Networks: Social Ties and Political News Production**

<details>

  <p>
  Do social connections with journalists help politicians gain media visibility and favorable coverage? Research on media bias has largely focused on the ideological or partisan roots of media slant, paying relatively less attention to the role that informal social ties between politicians and journalists play in shaping political news. I examine the influence of such connections on political coverage in national newspapers during South Korea's post-democratization period (1992–2007), using a novel biographical dataset that links Members of the National Assembly and journalists through shared birthplace and alumni affiliations. Analyzing newspaper coverage of the National Assembly's annual audit of state affairs, I find that alumni ties with editors amplify the prominent coverage that legislators receive from their audit activities, in particular for junior legislators. These findings shed light on a relatively under-investigated source of media slant in comparative contexts and have implications for understanding the conditions necessary for media independence more broadly.
  </p>
</details>



**Enough to be Coethnic? Loyalty Signaling and Coethnic Immigrant Inclusion** <em>(Under Review)</em>

  <summary>
    <strong>Abstract</strong>

       target="_blank"
       rel="noopener"
       onclick="event.stopPropagation()"
       style="margin-left: 20px;">Manuscript</a>
  </summary>

  <p>
  Shared ethnicity is widely expected to facilitate immigrant inclusion in ethnically homogeneous societies, yet coethnic immigrants sometimes face similar levels of discrimination as other immigrant groups. Under what conditions does coethnicity confer an advantage in immigrant inclusion? This article examines whether the inclusion of coethnic immigrants depends on their expressions of national loyalty, which may affirm or undermine their ethnicity-based claims to national belonging. Using original survey data from South Korea, I find that approximately 30 percent of native respondents demand greater patriotism from coethnic than from non-coethnic immigrants, and the strongest predictor of this stricter standard is the belief that common ancestry is an important criterion for national membership. To examine whether these expectations translate into differential treatment, I embed two experiments in the survey. In a hypothetical dictator game, respondents allocate more tokens to coethnic than to non-coethnic immigrants, but this advantage diminishes substantially when coethnic immigrants express support for their country of origin. By contrast, in a conjoint experiment on immigrants' access to welfare benefits, the coethnic advantage persists regardless of such expressions. These findings suggest that the advantages of shared ethnicity may depend on immigrants' expressions of loyalty, although this conditionality varies across different forms of immigrant inclusion.
  </p>
</details>


**American Military Broadcasting and Democratization**

<details>
  <summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>

  <p>
  Foreign media has been conventionally viewed as a threat to authoritarian stability, but recent research finds that the entertainment value of foreign content may instead increase support for authoritarian regimes. In this paper, I integrate and test these diverging arguments by exploiting a rare shift in the programming composition of the American Forces Korea Network (AFKN), which was an entertainment-focused television channel that was popular among South Korean citizens. Using novel data on AFKN broadcast transmitters and geo-coded election returns, I examine whether the sudden increase of journalistic programming in 1983 affected support for the military dictatorship between the 1981 and 1985 legislative elections. Preliminary evidence shows that the shift from entertainment to news led to a disproportionate drop in ruling party support and mobilized electoral participation in AFKN reception areas.
  </p>
</details>
